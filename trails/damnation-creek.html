Damnation Creek Trail may be the most dramatic hike in the entire California state parks system. In just 2.2 miles, the trail descends 1,100 feet from an old-growth redwood forest on the ridge above Highway 101 down through increasingly wild terrain to a completely hidden Pacific beach accessible by no other route. The contrast — ancient forest, then crashing surf — is extraordinary. The terrain is demanding, but the reward is absolute solitude and some of the most powerful scenery in Northern California.

The name is evocative and apt. The route was historically used by Indigenous Tolowa people and later by settlers trying to access the coast, and the difficulty of the descent apparently earned it its profane designation. Today that difficulty is a feature — it keeps casual visitors away and ensures the beach below remains as wild as the forest above.

This is genuinely hard hiking. The trail switchbacks relentlessly down steep, rooted slopes. The return trip is entirely uphill with 1,100 feet to regain. But those who complete it will stand on a hidden crescent of gravel beach, surrounded by rocky sea stacks, with old-growth redwoods visible on the headlands above, and not another person in sight.

The Descent Through Old Growth

The trail begins from a pull-out on the east side of Highway 101 — easy to miss if you're not watching for it. The first half-mile runs along the ridge through impressive old-growth trees before the real descent begins. The forest here is particularly intact and undisturbed; you're walking through what the entire California coast once looked like.

As you lose elevation, the forest composition shifts. Pure redwood stands give way to a mixed forest of Sitka spruce, western hemlock, and red alder — species that increasingly dominate near the fog-drenched coast. The trail becomes steeper, the switchbacks tighter, the roots more treacherous. Trekking poles are genuinely useful here — particularly on the return uphill climb.

The Hidden Beach

The beach at the trail's end is unlike anything else in California. It's a small, wild crescent of dark pebbles and sand, hemmed between sea stacks and headlands, with no facilities, no crowds, and no way to reach it except on foot. The surf here is powerful and the undertow can be dangerous — swimming is not recommended — but sitting on the rocks and watching the ocean is one of the most elemental experiences the redwood parks offer.

From December through April, the beach is an excellent spot for gray whale watching. Gray whales migrate along this coast in large numbers, and their spouts and breaching are visible from the headlands above. Bring binoculars. On a good December morning, you may see a dozen or more whales passing within a mile of shore.

Safety First

Do not swim at the beach — the surf is powerful with a strong undertow. The trail is steep and can be slippery when wet. Turn back if weather deteriorates. Always tell someone your plans and expected return time when hiking to remote locations.

Trail Logistics

  • TrailheadEast side of US-101, ~4 miles south of Crescent City
  • ParkingSmall pull-out, ~6 cars maximum
  • Day Use Fee$8/vehicle (CA State Park)
  • No FacilitiesNo water or restrooms at trailhead or beach
  • DogsLeashed permitted, but trail is very steep
  • Cell ServiceNone in canyon
  • Best ConditionsMay–October; slippery in wet weather

Essential Tips

  • • Trekking poles strongly recommended
  • • Start early — return uphill takes longer than expected
  • • Carry 2+ liters of water; no sources on trail
  • • Beach surf is dangerous — do not wade
  • • Dec–Apr: bring binoculars for gray whale watching
  • • Trail can be muddy and slippery after rain
