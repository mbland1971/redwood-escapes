Living Forest

An Extraordinary Ecosystem

The old-growth redwood forest is one of the most biodiverse ecosystems in North America. The life here is as remarkable as the ancient trees themselves.

Wildlife Viewing Guidelines

Keep Your DistanceStay 75+ feet from elk and 100+ feet from bears. Use binoculars — never approach for a better photo.
Never Feed WildlifeFeeding is illegal and harmful. Bears that associate food with people are relocated or destroyed.
Dawn & DuskMost wildlife is most active in the first and last hour of daylight. Early risers are consistently rewarded.
Be QuietMove slowly and speak softly. Noise drives animals away and disturbs their natural behavior.
Stay on TrailsOff-trail movement disturbs nesting birds and crushes ground-nesting insects and plants.
Leave No TracePack out all food waste. Food smells linger and attract bears to camping areas.
🦌

Roosevelt Elk

Cervus canadensis roosevelti

Roosevelt elk are North America's largest elk subspecies, and the redwood parks host one of their healthiest remaining populations. Bulls can weigh over 1,100 pounds and carry antlers spanning five feet. The herds at Elk Prairie in Prairie Creek Redwoods are among the most accessible in the world — you can observe them year-round from the Newton B. Drury Scenic Parkway.

The fall rut (September–November) is one of the most dramatic wildlife spectacles in the western United States. Bull elk bugle — a haunting, multi-toned call that carries for miles — while sparring for access to harems of up to 20 cows.

Viewing Tip: Stay 75+ feet away at all times. During rutting season bulls are unpredictable. Never exit your vehicle when elk are on or near the road.

Where to See:
Elk Prairie (Prairie Creek), Gold Bluffs Beach, Elk Meadow near Orick

Best Time:
Year-round; rut Sept–Nov; dawn and dusk most active

🐻

Black Bear

Ursus americanus

Black bears are common throughout all five redwood parks, though sightings remain relatively uncommon for the average visitor. The redwood ecosystem provides excellent bear habitat — diverse foraging opportunities, dense cover, and abundant streams. Bears here are typically shy and avoid humans, but food storage is strictly regulated.

Bears are most active in spring (emerging from winter dens) and late summer and fall (hyperphagia — intensive eating before denning). They are omnivores, feeding on berries, acorns, insects, and occasionally carrion or young ungulates.

Safety Tip: Store all food in bear canisters or designated lockers. If you see a bear: stand tall, make noise, back away slowly. Never run — it triggers a chase response.

Where to See:
Throughout all parks; Jedediah Smith and Humboldt Redwoods offer best odds

Best Time:
Spring through fall; dawn and dusk most active

🦦

North American River Otter

Lontra canadensis

River otters thrive in the clean, cold rivers and lagoons of the redwood parks — particularly along the Smith River, Klamath River, and coastal lagoons near Trinidad. They're highly social, playful animals that travel in family groups. Watching a family of otters fish and frolic in the turquoise Smith River is one of the great wildlife moments the parks offer.

Otters are powerful, agile swimmers that can remain submerged for up to 4 minutes. They play a crucial role in regulating fish and amphibian populations, making them an important indicator of aquatic ecosystem health.

Viewing Tip: Otters are shy but curious. Stay still and quiet at river banks and you may observe them fishing or grooming. Morning visits to the Smith River are most productive.

Where to See:
Smith River (Jedediah Smith), Klamath River mouth, Humboldt Lagoons

Best Time:
Year-round; most visible early morning

🦅

Marbled Murrelet

Brachyramphus marmoratus

The marbled murrelet is one of the redwood parks' most fascinating residents — a seabird that nests not on cliffs but on the wide, mossy branches of old-growth trees, sometimes miles inland. It's one of the only seabirds in North America to nest in old-growth forest, which is why old-growth protection is directly linked to this species' survival.

They are fast, darting fliers that commute between nesting trees and the ocean at dawn and dusk. Their haunting "keer keer keer" call at first light is one of the iconic sounds of the old-growth forest.

Viewing Tip: Stand still at the edge of old-growth groves before sunrise and listen for their distinctive calls as they fly overhead from their nesting trees toward the coast.

Where to See:
Old-growth zones throughout all parks; best heard at dawn

Best Time:
Breeding season April–July; present year-round

🐟

Steelhead Trout

Oncorhynchus mykiss

The Smith River is California's premier steelhead stream — the last major undammed river in the state, running cold and clear from the Klamath Mountains to the Pacific. Steelhead (ocean-going rainbow trout) return to spawn in the winter months, filling the cobblestone runs with fish up to 20 pounds.

Watching the spawning runs from riverside overlooks at Jedediah Smith is one of the parks' most underrated experiences. The fish are visible in the clear water, working upstream against the current with extraordinary power and determination.

Viewing Tip: Best viewing from the riverbanks at Jedediah Smith Campground and Stout Grove during October–March. A California fishing license is required if you want to fish.

Where to See:
Smith River (Jedediah Smith SP), Klamath River, Redwood Creek

Best Time:
October through March for spawning runs

🦎

Pacific Giant Salamander

Dicamptodon tenebrosus

The Pacific giant salamander is one of the largest terrestrial salamanders in North America, reaching up to 13 inches in length. They live in the cold, clear streams of the redwood forests — particularly in Fern Canyon — and are an important indicator species for water quality. Unlike most salamanders, they can make barking or rattling sounds when threatened.

Finding one in the gravel stream bed of Fern Canyon is a genuine thrill for wildlife enthusiasts. Their mottled brown-and-tan coloration provides excellent camouflage against stream substrate.

Viewing Tip: Look under rocks in stream beds, particularly in Fern Canyon. Never remove them from the water — the oils on human skin can harm them.

Where to See:
Fern Canyon stream bed, cold stream beds throughout all parks

Best Time:
Year-round; most active in cool, wet conditions

🐌

Banana Slug

Ariolimax columbianus

The banana slug is the beloved mascot of the California redwood forest — a bright yellow, occasionally spotted slug that can reach 10 inches in length and is the second-largest terrestrial slug species on Earth. Far from being a pest, banana slugs are crucial ecosystem decomposers, breaking down plant material and cycling nutrients through the forest floor.

Their bright yellow coloration serves as a warning signal — they produce a mucus that numbs the mouths of potential predators. The slime also acts as a natural adhesive, allowing them to climb vertical surfaces. They are harmless, slow-moving, and oddly endearing.

Viewing Tip: Watch where you step on forest trails after rain — banana slugs are common on the trail surface and easy to accidentally step on. They are most abundant after rainfall.

Where to See:
Forest floors throughout all parks, especially after rain

Best Time:
Year-round; most visible after rainfall

🐋

Gray Whale

Eschrichtius robustus

From December through April, thousands of gray whales migrate along the Northern California coast — one of the longest migrations of any mammal on Earth, 10,000 miles each way between feeding grounds in the Arctic and breeding lagoons in Baja California. The headlands above Del Norte Coast Redwoods are some of the finest whale-watching locations on the entire Pacific coast.

Mothers with calves pass closest to shore during the northward migration in March and April. Spouts are visible from 2+ miles offshore, and breaching behavior — full leaps from the water — occurs occasionally and is spectacular when it happens.

Viewing Tip: Scan the horizon methodically from coastal headlands. Look for the distinctive heart-shaped spout (gray whales have two blowholes that create a V-shape). Binoculars or a spotting scope are essential.

Where to See:
Del Norte Coast headlands, Klamath River Overlook, Trinidad Head

Best Time:
December–April; southward Dec–Jan, northward Feb–Apr

Wildlife Hotspots

Best Parks for Wildlife Viewing

Each park in the complex has its own wildlife specialties — here's where to go for each species.

🦌

Prairie Creek Redwoods

Best for elk — Elk Prairie meadow has year-round herds with fall rut viewing. Also excellent for black bear along the trail network.

🐟

Jedediah Smith

Best for river wildlife — otters, steelhead trout, and osprey along the pristine turquoise Smith River. Also best for Pacific giant salamanders.

🐋

Del Norte Coast

Best for marine wildlife — gray whale watching from the coastal headlands (Dec–Apr), harbor seals year-round, and rich tide pools.

🦅

Redwood National Park

Best for birdwatching — marbled murrelets at dawn, peregrine falcons on sea cliffs, and great blue herons along Redwood Creek.

🌿

Humboldt Redwoods

Best for forest floor diversity — banana slugs, salamanders, and rare plant species in the Rockefeller Forest old-growth.

🦆

Humboldt Lagoons SP

Best for waterfowl and shorebirds — the lagoons are critical habitat for migrating birds on the Pacific Flyway, including loons and grebes.
